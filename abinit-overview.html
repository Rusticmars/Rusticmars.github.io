ABINIT: an open-source engine for predicting what materials do

A.H. Romero, D.C. Allan, B. Amadon, et al., including N.A. Pike (43 authors in total). “ABINIT: Overview and Focus on Selected Capabilities.” The Journal of Chemical Physics 152, 124102 (2020).

View the paper at the publisher (DOI) Request a copy

In brief. ABINIT is a free, open-source program that computes the properties of materials from quantum mechanics. This paper is a community overview of the software: its history, what it can calculate, and a selection of capabilities that few comparable programs offer. The calculations behind several other pages on this site were carried out with it.

1. Background

What electronic-structure software does

The properties of a solid follow from the behavior of its electrons. Electronic-structure programs solve the quantum-mechanical equations for those electrons, most often using density functional theory (DFT), so that properties such as how a crystal conducts, vibrates, responds to light, or deforms under stress can be predicted from the atoms alone. ABINIT represents the electrons’ wave functions with plane waves (a periodic, wave-like basis suited to crystals), and replaces the tightly bound inner electrons of each atom with pseudopotentials or projector-augmented waves (PAW), which are simplified descriptions that make the calculation tractable. Molecules and nanostructures can also be treated, by placing them in a periodically repeated box.

The project

The code traces back to work in the late 1980s at Corning Incorporated and Cornell University, where Douglas Allan, supervised by Michael Teter, wrote an early plane-wave program. Xavier Gonze joined in 1990 and added density functional perturbation theory (DFPT), a method for computing how a material responds to small disturbances. In 1996 Gonze and Allan decided to create a freely licensed code, which was named ABINIT in September 1998, and the first public release under the GNU General Public License followed in December 1999. Today the code is developed by a community of around 50 people overseen by an international advisory committee. It is documented in detail, with more than 800 worked examples, a user forum, and regular schools and workshops.

Reach

A bibliometric count in December 2019 found 5,125 citations of the main ABINIT papers since 2002, of which 3,995 were unique, averaging more than 250 per year since 2011, from authors in 84 countries and more than 2,000 institutions.

Spotlight: Raman spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy measures how light scatters inelastically from the vibrations of a material, giving a fingerprint of its structure. In ABINIT, the intensity of a Raman line is obtained from the Raman tensor, which describes how the material’s electrical susceptibility changes as the atoms move. This is a third derivative of the energy, which the 2n+1 theorem allows to be computed from first-order responses at little additional cost:

∂χ(1)ij / ∂τκβ = −(1/Ω0) ∂³E / (∂τκβ ∂ℰi ∂ℰj),   αmij = Σκ,β (∂χ(1)ij / ∂τκβ) um(κβ)   (1)χ(1)ij: linear electric susceptibility (dimensionless in SI units of ε0); τκβ: displacement of atom κ along direction β (Å); Ω0: unit-cell volume (Å³); E: total energy (eV); ℰ: electric field; αmij: Raman tensor of vibrational mode m; um(κβ): eigen-displacement of that mode. Placzek’s approximation is assumed, and the formulation applies in the non-resonant regime.

Scripts distributed with ABINIT convert these tensors into polarization-dependent and powder-averaged Raman spectra. Calculated spectra have been compared with experiment for oxides, organic compounds, and transition-metal dichalcogenides, and the paper cites the author’s own studies of those layered crystals as an example (see the nonlinear-optics page for a related study carried out with ABINIT).

3. Community projects

Atomic data. The community distributes validated sets of atomic data: the JTH table of PAW data for 86 elements, and PseudoDojo, which offers standard and stringent pseudopotential sets whose convergence hints support automated workflows.

High-throughput calculations. Using the ABIPY and ABIFLOWS software to automate workflows, phonon spectra were computed for more than 2,000 materials and made available through the Materials Project. A second-harmonic-generation study had computed nonlinear properties for more than 400 of more than 800 candidate materials at the time of writing, and automated GW calculations had been applied to about 90 crystalline compounds.

A shared library. LIBPAW packages ABINIT’s PAW machinery so that other programs can reuse it. It has been integrated into at least two other codes, one using wavelets and one using Gaussian orbitals, which shows that it is independent of the underlying basis.

4. What this means

Predictions of material behavior are only as trustworthy as the software behind them. A freely available, openly developed, extensively tested code allows others to reproduce and build on published results, and allows new methods, such as those for Raman spectra and nonlinear optics, to be shared with the whole community rather than kept within one group.

5. Limitations

This is an overview of a large project, written by many authors, and it describes the software as of early 2020; some capabilities discussed were then available only in a forthcoming version, and the code has continued to develop since. Several implementations are restricted to certain pseudopotential types or exchange-correlation functionals, which the paper notes individually. This page summarizes selected parts of the paper only.
