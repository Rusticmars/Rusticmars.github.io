In brief. Crystals only a single layer thick can convert light of one color into light of exactly twice the frequency, and can change their refractive index when a voltage is applied. Measurements of these effects disagree widely, so this study calculated them from quantum mechanics for nine monolayer materials. The results are of comparable magnitude to bulk crystals used in devices today, which suggests that much thinner components may be possible.

1. Background

Nonlinear optics

In everyday optics, a material responds to light in proportion to the light’s strength. Intense light can also produce responses that are not proportional. The simplest of these is second-harmonic generation (SHG): two photons of energy ħω are absorbed and one photon of energy 2ħω is emitted, so the output light has twice the frequency of the input. (Frequency doubling is how many green laser pointers work, converting infrared light.) A related effect, the Pockels effect, is a change in a material’s refractive index in proportion to an applied electric field, and is the basis for electro-optic modulators that encode signals onto light.

Both effects are described by a second-order susceptibility, written χ(2), and both require a crystal that lacks inversion symmetry (a crystal that looks the same when every point is reflected through its center cannot show them).

Why monolayer transition-metal dichalcogenides

Transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are layered crystals with the formula MX2, in which a plane of transition-metal atoms (M) lies between two planes of chalcogen atoms (X = sulfur, selenium, or tellurium). A single layer has the required lack of inversion symmetry. Its symmetry group (D3h) leaves a single independent nonzero component of χ(2). Group VI TMDs (M = molybdenum or tungsten) are semiconductors whose SHG has been measured many times. Group V TMDs (M = vanadium or niobium) range from metallic to semiconducting and had no known SHG measurements. Reported values for the same material vary considerably, depending on the number of layers, defects, the substrate, and the method used to extract χ(2) from the measured signal. A first-principles reference, free of those complications, was therefore needed.

2. Method

Calculations used the open-source ABINIT software. Atomic structures were relaxed using density functional theory (DFT), with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) approximation plus the D3 van der Waals correction, and at least 20 Å of vacuum separated periodic copies of the single layer. Optical properties were computed from the electronic band structure following the band-theory formalism of Sipe and co-workers, in which the nonlinear response is the sum of three contributions (interband, intraband, and current terms), evaluated as a function of photon energy.

Because periodic calculations add vacuum, the cell volume is artificially large; results were corrected by the ratio of bulk to monolayer cell volumes (a factor φ) so that susceptibilities can be compared with experiments. The Pockels coefficient was obtained from the electro-optic susceptibility together with the calculated refractive index.

3. Results

Structures, gaps, and magnetism

Calculated lattice parameters agree with other DFT calculations to 0.5% on average and with available experiments to about 1%. The group VI materials have direct gaps, except WSe2, which is indirect, in agreement with experiments attributing the change to small residual stress. Among the group V materials, NbS2, VS2, and VSe2 are predicted to be magnetic semiconductors, and NbSe2 a semimetal.

The magnitudes are a different matter. The calculated χ(2) is larger than most experimental values, and the measurements themselves disagree considerably with one another. The study attributes much of this spread to how experiments extract the susceptibility: the model for the film, the substrate’s optical properties, and the assumed thickness of a layer one atom thick. Without the volume correction, the calculated values decrease, so the treatment of the vacuum also matters.

Trends and their origin

For a given transition metal, the susceptibility is largest when the chalcogen atom is largest. A larger atom enlarges the lattice, flattens the electronic bands, and lowers the band gap. The nonlinear response is strongest where pairs of bands run parallel with opposite slopes, because the energy separating such bands produces peaks in the spectrum. Smaller gaps and flatter bands thus both raise the response. This is consistent with a recently reported theoretical upper limit on χ(2) that scales inversely with the fourth power of the band gap: the vanadium compounds, with the smallest gaps, show the largest susceptibilities, and VSe2 peaks at lower energy than the other monolayers. MoTe2 has a susceptibility of the same order as bulk gallium arsenide (GaAs), a standard nonlinear crystal.

The Pockels effect

For the group VI monolayers, the calculated Pockels coefficient is roughly constant below the band gap and rises, approximately linearly, near the gap, with values equal to or larger than GaAs at low energies (GaAs: 1.86 pm/V at 1.413 eV). The vanadium compounds have larger coefficients that vary nonlinearly below 1 eV, and the niobium compounds have smaller ones. A coefficient that is constant over a range of photon energies could be useful for electro-optical devices in the near-infrared.

4. What this means

If a one-atom-thick crystal responds as strongly as a bulk crystal, an optical component could be made far thinner, allowing smaller electro-optic devices. The calculations also point experimentalists toward unexplored materials, in particular the vanadium compounds, whose strong response occurs at lower energies than in MoS2, and, being magnetic, may support combined electro-optical and magnetic functions.

5. Limitations

The calculations use an independent-particle picture and omit excitons, the bound electron-hole pairs that matter in monolayers; many-body calculations that include them were beyond the scope of the study. No correction was applied to the band gaps. Spin-orbit coupling was included in the band structures and linear optical response but not in the matrix elements for the nonlinear response, and convergence of the second-order response was limited by computer memory. For the metallic compounds the results are a first approximation, and the group V compounds were assumed to have the hexagonal ground state. Magnetic order was assumed to be ferromagnetic.
