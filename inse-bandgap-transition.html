Why thickness decides how indium selenide handles light

N.A. Pike, R. Pachter, M.A. Altvater, C.E. Stevens, M. Klein, J.R. Hendrickson, H. Zhang, S. Krylyuk, A.V. Davydov, N.R. Glavin. “Understanding the Origin and Implication of the Indirect-to-Direct Bandgap Transition in Multilayer InSe.” The Journal of Physical Chemistry C 128, 7957–7967 (2024).

View the paper at the publisher (DOI) Request a copy

In brief. Indium selenide (InSe) is a layered semiconductor whose ability to emit light efficiently depends on how many layers are stacked. This study combined quantum-mechanical calculations with low-temperature light-emission measurements to identify why: as layers are added, the interior layers compress, which shifts the electronic states that set the energy gap until the gap switches from “indirect” to “direct.”

1. Background

Layered crystals

The isolation of graphene, a single sheet of carbon atoms, opened the field of atomically thin materials. Many crystals are built from sheets with strong bonds inside each sheet and weak van der Waals (vdW) attraction between sheets, so they can be thinned to a few layers or a single layer. InSe is one such material. It has a high electron mobility, which makes it attractive for transistors, and promising optical properties. Its basic building block is a “quad-layer” of four atomic planes (selenium, indium, indium, selenium), and the way quad-layers are stacked defines several polytypes of the bulk crystal, named β, γ, δ, and ε. This work concentrates on the γ stacking.

Direct versus indirect gaps

In a semiconductor, electrons occupy a lower-energy valence band and, when excited, a higher-energy conduction band, separated by the energy gap. Each state has an associated momentum. If the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band occur at the same momentum, the gap is direct, and an electron can drop across it and emit a photon readily. If they occur at different momenta, the gap is indirect, and the transition also requires a change of momentum, typically supplied by lattice vibrations, which makes light emission far less efficient. InSe was known, experimentally and from earlier calculations, to change from an indirect to a direct gap as its thickness increases. What was missing was an explanation of the mechanism.

2. Method

Calculations. Density functional theory (DFT), with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) approximation and a van der Waals correction (D3), was used to relax the structures and compute band structures for monolayer, multilayer (two to ten layers), and bulk InSe, in the γ stacking. Standard DFT underestimates band gaps, so the fundamental gap was also computed with the G0W0 approximation and a hybrid functional (HSE). Optical absorption, including the electron-hole pairs called excitons, was computed by solving the Bethe-Salpeter equation on top of G0W0 (G0W0-BSE), with and without spin-orbit coupling (SOC).

Experiments. Single crystals of InSe were grown by the vertical Bridgman method. Powder X-ray diffraction and atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy confirmed the γ phase. Photoluminescence (PL), light emitted after laser excitation, was measured from 4 K to 300 K, and ellipsometry provided optical constants at room temperature.

3. Results

Bulk InSe: calculation meets experiment

For all four polytypes, the lowest absorption peak (labeled A) arises mainly from out-of-plane dipole contributions, meaning that it couples to light polarized perpendicular to the layers. The B and C peaks have both in-plane and out-of-plane contributions, with the out-of-plane part several times larger. In the measured γ-phase PL at 4 K, the A exciton appears at about 1.345 eV and the B exciton at about 2.078 eV; a third feature, the P-band, at 1.335 eV, arises from exciton-exciton scattering and allows the exciton binding energy to be estimated at about 10 meV, consistent with the commonly reported 15 meV. The calculated binding energy for γ-InSe is smaller (5 meV) but of comparable magnitude. The calculated A peak for the γ phase (0.95 eV) underestimates the measured value, and the authors note that fully consistent GW-BSE calculations might improve the prediction.

Monolayer: an indirect gap

The monolayer has an indirect gap. The top of the valence band lies at a momentum point (labeled Q) slightly away from the zone center Γ, giving the valence band a “Mexican-hat” profile, while the bottom of the conduction band lies at Γ. The monolayer’s exciton binding energy is calculated to be 0.275 eV, much larger than in bulk because of quantum confinement and weaker electrical screening, and comparable in order of magnitude to monolayer MoS2 and MoTe2. The calculated monolayer thickness of 0.84 nm agrees with measured values of about 0.8 to 0.87 nm.

Multilayers: the mechanism

Including the van der Waals correction proved essential: without it, calculated lattice parameters diverge as layers are added, and with it they converge. The central structural finding is that, in a stack, the interior layers compress toward their bulk dimensions (the In–Se bonds shorten, while In–In bonds remain essentially constant), whereas the outer layers stay “monolayer-like.” This differs from layered transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), where compression is driven by interactions between the chalcogen atoms; in InSe it is driven by the metal-chalcogen bonds.

As the stack thickens, the momentum point Q moves toward Γ and the character of the electronic states changes: the top valence band, built mainly from selenium pz orbitals (orbitals pointing perpendicular to the layers), drops in energy below the bands built from px and py orbitals. Together these changes bring about the transition to a direct gap. Bond lengths and orbital contributions are correlated, with Pearson correlations of 0.78 between the In–In bond length and the valence-band indium pz contribution, and 0.83 between the In–Se bond length and the valence-band selenium pz contribution.

The two higher-level methods bracket the experimental value, whereas standard DFT alone places the transition at implausibly large thicknesses. The authors emphasize that accounting for electronic correlation, and benchmarking the level of theory against experiment, is essential for this question. Calculated gaps as a function of thickness were fitted to an exponential form, and a simple quantum-well model was found to describe thicknesses above about 5 nm but not below it, where quantum confinement dominates.

4. What this means

Thickness is a design variable. In InSe, the number of layers determines not only the size of the energy gap but also whether the gap is direct, and therefore how efficiently the material emits and absorbs light. Identifying the structural origin of that switch, the compression of interior layers and its effect on the pz-derived electronic states, provides a basis for designing few-layer InSe heterostructures for transistors and electro-optical devices.

5. Limitations

The calculations treat idealized model systems that do not fully reproduce experimental conditions. Band-gap curves were shifted by a scissor correction of about 0.9 eV to reproduce the known bulk gap. The G0W0 calculations were single-shot and used a reduced number of momentum points because of memory and time limits, and fully consistent GW-BSE calculations are beyond the scope of the study, as are measurements of exciton binding energy in multilayer InSe.
